 1            Introduction.1 Meta devotes substantial attention to criticizing the potential size of the AGs’

 2   requested monetary relief. In doing so, Meta misstates the law and ignores evidence of the vast

 3   scope and gravity of its unlawful conduct. Pursuant to Court Order, ECF 462 (3244) at 1, the AGs

 4   focus this Reply on one of Meta’s erroneous arguments: that the AGs request double recovery. 2

 5            Meta’s concern over the potential size of the requested remedies rings hollow in light of the

 6   evidence the AGs will present at trial. The AGs will prove that Meta deceived the public about the

 7   safety of its platforms while deliberately designing them to induce compulsive use and increase

 8   revenue. For the four AGs bringing consumer protection claims in the August 2026 trial, the

 9   evidence will show almost 40 million monthly instances of teen users spending more than 2 hours

10   per day, on average, on Instagram between January 2013 and March 2024—amounting to each

11   such teen user spending roughly 60 hours per month on Instagram alone. AGs’ Submission, Ex. B.

12   And despite Meta’s repeated claims that children under 13 (“U13s”) were not permitted on its

13   platforms, the evidence will show that, from 2012 to 2023, more than 4.6 million U13s used

14   Instagram and 3.9 million U13s used Facebook in those same four States. Id., Ex. A. Meta’s

15   unlawful conduct poses serious risks for the development and mental health of youth who

16   compulsively use its platforms, with lasting consequences for their families and communities.

17            The AGs do not seek double recovery. The AGs do, however, insist on substantial remedies,

18   as provided for under the law, in order to effectively punish, deter, and strip Meta—a company that

19   accrued more than $200 billion in revenue in 2025 alone—of its ill-gotten gains from its unlawful

20   activity. Ex. 1, Meta Platforms, Inc., Form 10-K, at 61 (Jan. 28, 2026).

23   in the AGs’ Submission on remedies. The AGs do not seek, and will not request, excessive recovery

24   beyond what the law permits. The AGs are also mindful that the Court will not grant remedies that

 2            Meta misstates the law. Meta’s premise—that penalties are duplicative unless violations

 3   are limited to the number of offending acts or the number of people who viewed or relied upon

 4   deceptive statements—is incorrect. See AGs’ Submission at 8–9 (citing supportive cases and

 5   statutes). When counting violations, the factfinder looks to the evidence and “circumstances of the

 6   case,” and accounts for the reach, duration, repetition, or severity of the misconduct—not individual

 7   injury, loss, or reliance. People v. JTH Tax, Inc., 212 Cal. App. 4th 1219, 1251 (2013); see, e.g.,

 8   May Dep’t Stores Co. v. State ex rel. Woodard, 863 P.2d 967, 975 (Colo. 1993) (counting a

 9   violation for each day that a deceptive advertisement appeared); People v. Johnson & Johnson, 77

10   Cal. App. 5th 295, 354 (2022) (violation counts need only be “reasonably related to the gain or the

11   opportunity for gain achieved by the dissemination of the . . . deceptive advertisement” (internal

12   quotation omitted)). Meta’s cases are inapposite, as they either do not pertain to statutory civil

13   penalties or are limited to their facts. See, e.g., Granelli v. Chicago Title Ins. Co., 2012 WL

14   2072648, at *11-12 (D.N.J. June 8, 2012) (equitable relief was duplicative where “direct

15   damage[s]” wholly compensated the plaintiff for the economic injury); Ptaszynski v. Atl. Health

16   Sys., Inc., 111 A.3d 111, 120 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 2015) (discussing damages, not statutory

17   civil penalties); Lexton-Ancira Real Estate Fund, 1972 v. Heller, 826 P.2d 819, 823 (Colo. 1992)

18   (same); Johnson & Johnson, 77 Cal. App. at 354 (“[s]ubsequent decisions interpreting Olson have

19   concluded that . . . total circulation can be a reasonable method to determine the number of statutory

20   violations”); People v. Ashford Univ., LLC, 100 Cal. App. 5th 485, 515 (2024) (California “courts

21   have limited Jayhill to its facts”). Meta also ignores that under KRS 367.990, each unlawful act or

22   practice is a separate violation as to each consumer to whom it was directed and each transaction

23   in which it was employed. Finally, Meta’s due process argument is wholly speculative.

       Remedy Chart 3 does not double count Meta’s violations, as Meta suggests. Meta’s Submission
24   at 25–26. As Meta concedes, violations can be counted based on each “separate, affirmative act.”
     Id. at 10. COPPA prohibits “collect[ing] personal information from a child” without providing the
25   required protections. 15 U.S.C. § 6502(a)(1). This Court has also recognized that “keep[ing] or
     us[ing]” children’s data after obtaining actual knowledge violates COPPA. ECF 440 (3214) at 35.
26   Thus, each time Meta collected, used, or maintained each piece of personal information from a
     child after obtaining actual knowledge, Meta violated COPPA, and by extension, the state
27   consumer protection laws. Despite this multitude of violative acts against each child, Remedy
     Chart 3 counts only one violation for each child (or child’s account) affected by the unlawful act
28   identified in Remedy Charts 3.A-3.D. Thus the AGs undercount Meta’s actual knowledge
     violations, even if some of the same children are represented in more than one of Charts 3.A-3.D.
