 1                                                 INTRODUCTION
 2             Trial in this case is confined to the AGs’ claims (1) that specific, identified statements by Meta
 3   were false or misleading, (2) that three specific design features were unfair practices, and (3) that Meta
 4   violated COPPA because it had “actual knowledge” of under-13 users or its services were “directed to
 5   children.” This case is not an amorphous challenge over addiction or teen mental health untethered to
 6   those specific claims. For this reason, the AGs’ claimed remedies are limited to UPA violations—if
 7   proven—arising out of the claimed deceptive statements, unfair features, and alleged COPPA violations.
 8             Despite this, the AGs have not linked their claimed remedies to their alleged violations. They
 9   instead seek over one trillion dollars in penalties and disgorgement based on calculations that sweep in,
10   and double count, every single teen who uses Meta’s platforms, and every single month in which a teen
11   uses Meta’s platforms for more than a half-hour. See State AGs’ Penalty and Disgorgement Charts and
12   Supporting Materials (“Mot.”), ECF 444 (3218). These remedies have no basis in the record in this case,
13   are entirely unmoored from the claimed deceptive statements or unfair practices, are based on features
14   this Court has held are immune from liability under Section 230, and violate the legal and due process
15   limits on the scope of UPA penalties. For these reasons, and the reasons set forth below, Meta respectfully
16   requests that the Court grant the relief specified at the end of this brief.

 3          While courts decide “what amounts to a violation on a case-by-case basis,” People v. Johnson &
 4   Johnson, 77 Cal. App. 5th 295, 352 (2022); Mot. at 3, that determination is governed by case law
 5   establishing that the counting of UPA violations must be tied or linked to the conduct giving rise to the
 6   violation. In particular, the case law recognizes two general approaches to counting UPA violations:
 7   based either on (a) the number of consumers “exposed to” or affected by the defendant’s violations, or (b)
 8   the defendant’s affirmative unlawful acts. See, e.g., People v. Super. Ct. (Jayhill Corp.), 9 Cal. 3d 283,
 9   289 (1973) (“the number of violations is to be determined by the number of persons to whom the
10   misrepresentations were made”); May Dep’t. Stores Co. v. State ex rel. Woodard, 863 P.2d 967, 974 (Colo.
11   1993) (in CCPA case, “defin[ing] separate violations in terms of the dissemination of false and misleading
12   information”) (emphasis in original); Am. Nat’l Univ. of Ky., Inc. v. Commonwealth ex rel. Beshear, 2019
13   WL 2479608, at *7 (Ky. Ct. App. June 14, 2019) (unpublished) (employing second approach); Kugler v.
14   Romain, 58 N.J. 522, 533, 279 A.2d 640, 646 (1971) (same). Both approaches link the counting of
15   violations to the conduct that violated the statute.
16          Under the first approach, courts count violations by the number of consumers exposed to or
17   affected by the defendant’s violative acts. In People v. Superior Court (Olson), 96 Cal. App. 3d 181, 198
18   (1979), the California Court of Appeal held that “[a] single publication constitutes a minimum of one
19   violation with as many additional violations as there are persons who read the advertisement or who
20   responded to the advertisement.” (emphases added). The court declined to measure violations by the
21   “circulation of the newspaper” because it would be “‘unreasonable’ to assume that the Legislature
22   contemplated penalties of that magnitude for a false advertisement in a single edition of a newspaper.” Id.
23   at 196–97. So too in People v. Bestline Products, Inc., 61 Cal. App. 3d 879, 903 (1976), where the court
24   counted the “number of victims to which misrepresentations were made by each defendant,” and found
25   3,000 violations, because “false and misleading representations [were] made to upward of 10,000
26   prospective distributors, 3,000 of whom in fact acted thereon to become direct or general distributors.”
27   Id. at 923. Other courts have counted violations by the sales of misleading coupons, People v. Toomey,
28   157 Cal. App. 3d 1, 23 (1984), or the number of consumers who “spoke with” defendants’ sales

 3          Under the second approach, courts count violations based on the “separate, affirmative act or
 4   decisions by the defendant” that constitutes an “act of misfeasance” under applicable law. Am. Nat’l
 5   Univ., 2019 WL 2479608, at *6–7. In Am. Nat’l Univ., the Kentucky Court of Appeal “provide[d] …
 6   guidance on how trial courts may impose sanctions ‘per violation’ under the KCPA for material published
 7   online,” holding that “every KCPA violation must be based on separate, affirmative act[s] or decisions by
 8   the defendant” and “that in addition to the original publication, additional KCPA violations may be found
 9   whenever a defendant edits or adds substantive material … related to the information originally found to
10   run afoul of the KCPA.” Id. at *7–8. The California Court of Appeal applied the same logic in People v.
11   Ashford University LLC, affirming the trial court’s decision “to count each deceptive telephone call made
12   by defendants as a separate violation.” 100 Cal. App. 5th 485, 500 (2024); see also Johnson, 77 Cal. App.
13   5th at 352 (“count[ing] each deceptive … marketing communication as a separate violation”). And in
14   People ex rel. Kennedy v. Beaumont Inv., Ltd., 111 Cal. App. 4th 102, 128, 130 (2003), the trial court
15   “count[ed] each of defendants’ discrete unlawful acts as a separate statutory violation,” awarding penalties
16   for “each time defendants forced a tenant to accept the conditions of a long-term dealer lease.” The New
17   Jersey Supreme Court applied the same reasoning in Romain, 58 N.J. at 533, where the trial court held
18   “that since the 24 contracts involved in the action were obtained through practices violative of [NJ CFA],
19   a penalty of $100 in each instance or $2400 would be imposed upon defendant.” See also Woodard, 863
20   P.2d at 974 (“defin[ing] separate violations in terms of the dissemination of false and misleading
21   information”) (emphasis in original).
22          Under either approach, courts tie the number of violations to the defendant’s violative conduct.
23   This is inherent in the word “violation” itself. See Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining
24   “violation” as the “act of breaking or dishonoring the law”). Awarding for a “violation” without reference
25   to the conduct giving rise to it would ignore the clear meaning of the term. See Edgerly v. City & Cnty.
26   of San Francisco, 713 F.3d 976, 984 (9th Cir. 2013) (“[I]t is a settled principle of statutory construction,
27   that courts should strive to give meaning to every word in a statute.”) (citation omitted).

 1          Imposing penalties that are untethered to the alleged violation would also run afoul of due process
 2   rights. The principle that the punishment imposed must derive from the legal wrong “is deeply rooted and
 3   frequently repeated in common-law jurisprudence.” Solem v. Helm, 463 U.S. 277, 284 (1983); see also
 4   BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 575 & n.24 (1996). The form of the “violation” may vary
 5   with the nature of the case, but the penalty must always be tied to the defendant’s violative acts. See, e.g.,
 6   Ashford, 100 Cal. App. 5th at 500 (“count[ing] each deceptive telephone call made by defendants as a
 7   separate violation”) (emphasis added).
 8          None of the cases the AGs cite counts violations in a manner untethered to the defendant’s
 9   proscribed conduct. Each of those cases discussing violation-counting links the penalty to the underlying
10   legal wrong: counting the “number of victims to which misrepresentations were made by each defendant,”
11   Bestline, 61 Cal. App. 3d at 903; counting “each consumer” who “spoke with” defendants’ sales
12   representatives or “received [defendants’] marketing materials,” when the sales script and materials were
13   deceptive, Sarpas, 225 Cal. App. 4th at 1567; calculating penalties based on the “number of persons
14   directly affected by each act of unfair competition,” People v. Nat’l Ass’n of Realtors, 155 Cal. App. 3d
15   578, 585–86 (1984) (emphasis added); counting penalties based on “each unlawful offer and sale” by
16   defendants that violated securities laws, Platkin v. Kizito, 337 A.3d 354, 357 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div.
17   2025); counting violations based on the number of “misleading and deceptive contracts” entered into and
18   “false repair invoices” sent by defendant, People v. Dollar Rent–A–Car Systems, Inc., 211 Cal. App. 3d
19   119, 132 (1989); and affirming award of penalties based on “549 ads” taken out by defendants and
20   “thirteen representative consumers,” “twelve of whom testified to having seen such ads,” People v. Shifrin,
21   342 P.3d 506, 523 (Colo. App. 2014).
22          Many of the cases cited by the AGs do not address civil penalties or disgorgement at all. See, e.g.,
23   Prata v. Super. Ct., 91 Cal. App. 4th 1128, 1146 (2001) (ability of private UCL plaintiff to bring
24   representative action); Meshinsky v. Nichols Yacht Sales, Inc., 541 A.2d 1063, 1067 (N.J. 1988) (award
25   of “cover” damages and prejudgment interest to NJ CFA private plaintiff); Stevens v. Motorists Mut. Ins.
26   Co., 759 S.W.2d 819, 821 (Ky. 1988) (meaning of “service” under KCPA); Kimmelman v. Henkels &
27   McCoy, Inc., 527 A.2d 1368, 1375 (N.J. 1987) (relief available under New Jersey Antitrust Act). And the
28   cases cited by the AGs that discuss factors courts may consider in setting the amount of the civil penalty
